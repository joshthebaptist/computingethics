## Slide 4

- The Software Industry and Piracy
- Software piracy: copying, distributing or using software without the permission (licence) of the copyright owner.
- Costly to build
- Software needs large investment in research and development
- Protected by law
- Because it is costly, software is protected by copyright law and licences
- Cheap to copy
- The same high price tempts people to copy instead of buy
- A lasting problem
- As long as there are computers and software, piracy remains an issue

## Slide 5

- What Are the Implications of Piracy?
- 1. Economic
- Lost revenue for developers, lower tax income, fewer jobs and less investment in new software
- 2. Moral
- Taking someone’s work without permission or payment; weakens respect for others’ effort and property
- 3. Creators
- Years of creative work go unrewarded, discouraging innovation — especially for small local developers

## Slide 6

- Most Common Forms of Software Piracy
- Counterfeiting
- Copying software and selling it as if genuine
- Softlifting
- Buying one licence and installing or sharing it on many computers
- Hard-disk loading
- Selling computers with pirated software pre-installed
- Online piracy
- Torrents, “cracked” apps, modded APKs, key generators and illegal streaming (IPTV) boxes
- Licence overuse
- More users on a network or cloud service than the licence allows
- Trial & shareware misuse
- Using trials past their limit or bypassing activation

## Slide 7

- Why Some People Pirate: Pirate Culture
- Hobby and collecting
- Done as a hobby: collecting and trying out the latest programs
- Networks of people who share the same interest — forums, Discord and Telegram groups
- “Sharing is caring”
- Sharing software to study, to learn, or just for enjoyment
- Pirates often see themselves as helping others, not stealing
- Question: does a good motive (sharing) make the action (copying without permission) right?

## Slide 8

- Categories of Piracy by Who Does It
- Commercial pirates
- Businesses produce and sell illegal copies of popular software packages
- Organisations
- Businesses, agencies or schools buy one or a few licences but install them on many computers
- Online distributors
- Upload licensed software to websites, cloud drives or file-sharing groups for others to download

## Slide 12

- The Dangers of Pirated Software (1)
- 1. It leaves you open to attack. Cracked software and key generators are a common way to spread malware. BSA estimates malware from unlicensed software costs companies nearly US$359 billion a year.
- Risks you are exposed to
- Financial and confidential data
- Trade secrets
- Customers’ personal records
- Identity theft
- Ransomware, data loss and destruction
- Source: BSA Global Software Survey 2018

## Slide 13

- The Dangers of Pirated Software (2)
- More dangers
- 2. It may stop working when you need it most
- 3. It can lead to fines or prosecution
- 4. It cannot be safely updated or patched
- 5. It puts hardworking developers out of work
- Legal alternatives
- Free or student versions (e.g. Microsoft 365 for students, Autodesk education licences, GitHub Student Developer Pack)
- Cheaper editions or subscriptions
- Free and open-source software (next slide)
- Check if your university provides licences

## Slide 15

- The Effects of Piracy
- Losses for developers
- Companies that spent years developing software lose income
- Higher prices
- Makers raise prices to cover losses, so honest buyers pay more
- Less innovation
- Lower profits mean less investment, fewer new products and less interest in the software industry
- No support
- Pirated copies get no technical support, warranty or upgrades; they risk viruses and incompatibility
- Debate: some argue “if piracy only hurts the industry, it is not a moral issue.” Do you agree? Who else is harmed?

## Slide 16

- Licensing
- If you are not the owner, you need a licence: a legal document stating your right to use a piece of software. Most licences are agreements (EULAs) you accept when installing.
- A licence may give you the right to…
- Use
- Run the software as an end user
- Modify
- Change it by adding or removing code, and act as a producer
- Transfer
- Pass it on, or act as a collector, distributor or seller

## Slide 17

- Types of Software Licence Today
- Proprietary (EULA)
- Buy the right to use; no copying or changes (e.g. Windows)
- Subscription / SaaS
- Pay monthly or yearly; access ends if you stop (e.g. Adobe CC, Microsoft 365)
- Shareware / trial
- Try free for a limited time or with limited features
- Freeware
- Free to use, but the code is closed
- Open source
- Free to use, study and change: copyleft (GPL) or permissive (MIT, Apache)
- Creative Commons
- Mostly for content: owners choose which uses they allow

## Slide 18

- The Problems Behind Piracy
- Poor licence management
- Mistakes in licensing: e.g. an institution buys a licence but does not get permission for extra users or a new branch
- No effort to upgrade or transfer licences properly
- Not understanding or valuing ownership rights
- Attitudes that excuse it
- “Copying is not a big offence”
- “Using pirated software isn’t wrong”
- “It’s rude to tell others what they can and can’t do with software”
- “I got it from someone else — if they don’t care, why should I?”

## Slide 20

- The Dilemmas
- How do we tell copying for personal benefit from copying for learning?
- Is it sensible to fine a student who copies software for an assignment? How much?
- Nations that develop software vs nations that cannot afford original software
- Developers who want maximum legal protection vs groups who want IP protection minimised

## Slide 21

- The Basis for Debate
- Why we should pay
- Buyers of originals pay for development; pirates do not, so costs are never recovered
- What moral right does a pirate have to take software that cost so much to build?
- Technology is part of modern culture — does that make it free?
- Many people earn their living from software
- Why society tolerates piracy
- Cheaper access to software
- A chance to try before buying
- Speeds up computer literacy in developing countries
- Too much protection may leave developing countries behind
- “Information should be free to share”

## Slide 23

- Piracy, Culture and Law: The Western View
- Modern IP laws (copyright, patent, trademark) developed in the West — e.g. Britain’s Statute of Anne (1710) and the Berne Convention (1886)
- Every creation gets copyright protection to recognise and reward the creator
- Protects all creative workers: artists, authors, reporters, photographers, designers and craftspeople
- Core idea
- Creativity is property: the creator decides who may copy it, and is paid for it.

## Slide 24

- Piracy, Culture and Law: The Eastern View
- Many Asian traditions stress that creators have a social responsibility to share their work with society
- Sharing and collaboration are encouraged and respected
- Knowledge-sharing and helping others are highly valued
- Those with greater knowledge should help those with less
- Studies have linked such values to different attitudes to copying (Swinyard, Rinne & Kau, 1990)
- Handle with care
- These are broad cultural tendencies, not rules for every person. Today Japan, South Korea, China and Malaysia are major producers of IP and have strong copyright laws.

## Slide 25

- Is Copyright a Western Monopoly?
- Some in developing countries have argued that copyright is a Western concept that keeps the West’s monopoly over producing and distributing knowledge (Altbach, 1988).
- Debate continues over whether copyright can survive as copying gets ever easier
- Supporters call for copyright laws to be updated, not abandoned
- New ways to pay make legal access easier — subscriptions, app stores, streaming and micro-payments
- International rules now apply almost everywhere: TRIPS (1994) and the WIPO Copyright Treaty (1996)

## Slide 26

- Why Does Piracy Occur?
- Easy to copy
- One click, and the copy works exactly like the original
- Low awareness
- People don’t know copyright laws and rules
- Weak enforcement
- Laws are not always enforced
- To save money
- Originals seem too expensive
- Seen as acceptable
- “Everyone does it, and it’s not for profit”
- “Not really stealing”
- The owner still has the original, so no one seems harmed

## Slide 28

- Control Measures
- 1. Update the law
- The UK’s Federation Against Software Theft (FAST, 1984) lobbied for the Copyright (Computer Software) Amendment Act 1985 — software piracy became clearly illegal
- 2. Anti-piracy campaigns
- BSA | The Software Alliance and SIIA run awareness and enforcement campaigns; enforcement works best with education
- 3. Education
- Teach users about copyright laws and why copyright and trademarks matter
- 4. Technical protection
- Product activation, online licensing, subscriptions and cloud software make copying harder
- 5. Software asset management
- Organisations audit the software they use to stay licensed and secure

## Slide 29

- Piracy and the Law in Malaysia
- Copyright Act 1987
- Computer programs are protected as literary works
- Copying, selling or distributing pirated software is an offence
- Enforced by the Ministry of Domestic Trade and Cost of Living (KPDN)
- Copyright (Amendment) Act 2022
- New section 43AA: selling illicit streaming devices (pirate TV boxes) and apps is a crime
- Fine of RM10,000–RM200,000, up to 20 years’ jail, or both
- 2023: a student was fined for having an unauthorised link to a local TV drama; sellers of pre-loaded Android TV boxes were fined up to RM33,000

## Slide 30

- Controversial Issues
- A. Unique nature
- Programmers say problems remain despite the law, because software is a unique creative work
- B. Hard to trace
- The original stays in place when copied, making detection hard; some countries do little
- C. Idea vs copy
- Stolen ideas can be rebuilt as “new” software — how much can be taken before it is plagiarism?
- D. Unsure of the law
- Some developers never take action because they are unsure of their rights
- E. Unaware it is theft
- Many users don’t realise copying is a form of stealing
- F. Protects code, not ideas
- Copyright does protect software code, but not the underlying ideas or concepts

## Slide 31

- Which Law Should Protect Software?
- Option
- What it protects
- Limits
- Copyright
- The written code (as a literary work) — automatic, no registration needed
- Does not protect the idea or how the program works
- Patent
- A new technical invention implemented in software
- Software “as such” is generally not patentable (e.g. in Europe); US courts narrowed software patents (Alice, 2014); costly, and the invention must be published
- Trade secret
- Hidden source code and algorithms
- Lost once the secret leaks
- Licence / contract
- How users may use the software
- Only binds people who agree to it
- Who owns it? Usually the employer owns software written by employees as part of their job — unless a contract says otherwise.

## Slide 32

- New Frontier: AI and Piracy
- NEW
- Case: Bartz v. Anthropic (US). Authors sued AI company Anthropic for downloading pirated books to build training data for its Claude models. In 2025 a judge ruled that training AI on books was fair use, but keeping millions of pirated copies was not. A US$1.5 billion settlement — about US$3,000 per book — received final approval in July 2026.
- Questions it raises
- Is training AI on copyrighted work fair — or piracy?
- Does it matter how the data was obtained?
- Should creators be paid when AI learns from their work?
- Everyday AI piracy
- Sharing paid AI accounts or using “cracked” AI tools
- Using AI to copy someone’s code or design
- AI-generated code may contain licensed code — check before using it

## Slide 33

- Key Takeaways
- Piracy is copying, sharing or using software without a licence
- It harms developers, honest buyers — and the pirate, through malware and legal risk
- Malaysia’s Copyright Act protects software, and the 2022 amendment targets illegal streaming
- Free, student and open-source options remove most excuses for piracy
- New questions about AI and copyright are still being decided
