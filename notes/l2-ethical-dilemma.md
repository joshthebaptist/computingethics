## Slide 4

- What Is an Ethical Dilemma?
- A situation where a person must choose between two or more actions, each supported by an ethical reason — so whichever option is chosen, some value, right or duty is compromised.
- A decision-maker
- Someone must act — doing nothing is also a choice
- Real alternatives
- At least two possible courses of action
- Conflicting values
- Each option honours one value and sacrifices another

## Slide 5

- Temptation or Dilemma?
- Rushworth Kidder, How Good People Make Tough Choices (1995)
- Right vs wrong
- A moral temptation — e.g. copying a friend’s code. The answer is clear; the challenge is doing it.
- Right vs right
- A true dilemma — both options are defensible. This is what the four-step process is for.
- Four common right-vs-right patterns
- Truth vs loyalty
- Report a friend’s wrongdoing?
- Individual vs community
- One person’s needs vs the group’s
- Short term vs long term
- Quick fix vs lasting impact
- Justice vs mercy
- Apply the rules or show compassion?

## Slide 6

- Ethical Dilemmas in Computing Today
- Security flaw
- You find a serious vulnerability in your employer’s app. Report it publicly, or trust management that has ignored it for months?
- AI in hiring
- An AI screening tool saves time but seems to reject more women. Keep using it while it is fixed, or stop now?
- Employee monitoring
- Software could detect data theft but also reads staff messages. Is the protection worth the loss of privacy?
- A colleague’s shortcut
- A teammate pastes licensed code into your product to meet a deadline. Report them, or fix it quietly?

## Slide 8

- Four Steps for Ethical Analysis and Decision Making
- Adapted from Kallman & Grillo (1996)
- 1
- Understand the situation
- Facts, ethical issues, harms and stakeholders
- 2
- Isolate the dilemma
- State it as: Should X do Y?
- 3
- Analyse both alternatives
- Utilitarianism, rights & duties, Kant
- 4
- Decide and plan
- Defend the decision; plan its implementation
- Ethics is a practical science: the goal is not just analysis, but a definite judgement on what ought to be done.

## Slide 9

- Step 1: Understand the Situation
- STEP 1 OF 4
- a
- List and number the relevant facts
- b
- Which of these facts raise an ethical issue?
- c
- Why? Explain the issue
- d
- What is the potential or resulting harm?
- e
- List the stakeholders involved
- Tip
- Stakeholders include everyone affected by the decision — not only those who made it.

## Slide 10

- Step 2: Isolate the Major Ethical Dilemma
- STEP 2 OF 4
- What is the ethical dilemma that must be resolved NOW?
- Should [someone] do or not do [something]?
- Do
- State only the dilemma, as a yes/no question about one action by one person
- Don’t
- Give any reasons or analysis here — save them for Step 3

## Slide 11

- Step 3: Analyse Both Alternatives
- Answer each lettered question — you will cite the letters in Step 4
- STEP 3 OF 4
- If the action IS done
- If the action is NOT done
- Which is preferable?
- Utilitarian: harm
- A. Who is harmed?
- B. Who is harmed?
- C. Least harm: A or B
- Utilitarian: benefit
- D. Who benefits?
- E. Who benefits?
- F. Most benefit: D or E
- Rights & duties
- G. Which rights are abridged and which duties neglected? Identify the stakeholder, and pair each right with its duty
- Kant: respect
- H. Who is disrespected?
- I. Who is disrespected?
- J. H or I
- Kant: fairness
- K. Who is treated unlike others?
- L. Who is treated unlike others?
- M. K or L
- Kant: universality
- N. Benefits if everyone did it?
- O. Benefits if nobody did it?
- P. N or O

## Slide 12

- Step 4: Decide and Plan the Implementation
- STEP 4 OF 4
- A
- Make a defensible decision
- Answer the Step 2 question. Cite the letters from Step 3 that support you, and explain why those principles take priority where rights or duties conflict.
- B
- Plan the steps
- List the specific actions needed to carry out the decision.
- C
- Show the impact
- Explain how each major stakeholder is affected by these actions.
- D
- Prevent it in future
- Longer-term political, legal, technical, social or organisational changes.
- E
- Find the pivot point
- What should have been done, or not done, to avoid the dilemma in the first place?

## Slide 14

- Case 1: Levity or Libel?
- An e-mail-based effort to boost morale backfires
- LowRider Tractor Corporation must cut payroll in every division by 10% in six months; morale is at an all-time low
- Software engineer Bill Brundle writes a crude memo mocking management, signs it “Management”
- He bypasses the security software to e-mail it anonymously; it spreads across the company
- Bill denies knowing the author; his friend Betty, who knew of the idea, stays silent
- Director Harriet Corrigan has technicians trace the memo; Bill admits it
- Harriet’s problem
- Should she fire Bill? His job is crucial — but Betty could do it, and Betty’s own job is about to be eliminated.

## Slide 15

- Step 1: Relevant Facts
- WORKED EXAMPLE
- 1
- Deep, fast layoffs (10% in six months) made little room for fairness; morale is very low
- 2
- Bill wrote a memo mocking management, with offensive language, and signed it “Management”
- 3
- Bill bypassed system security to send it anonymously
- 4
- Co-workers forwarded it company-wide; some found it offensive
- 5
- Bill lied when asked who wrote it; Betty knew and said nothing
- 6
- Harriet traced the memo; Bill admitted it when confronted
- 7
- Bill’s job is crucial; Betty could replace him, and her own job is due to be cut

## Slide 16

- Step 1: Ethical Issues, Harms and Stakeholders
- WORKED EXAMPLE
- Fact
- Ethical issue
- Harm
- 2
- Impersonation and disrespect of management
- Damaged trust and reputation; offended staff
- 3
- Unauthorised bypass of security
- Weakened system security; possible offence under the Computer Crimes Act 1997
- 5
- Dishonesty (Bill) and silence (Betty)
- Loss of trust among colleagues
- 7
- Conflict of interest in deciding Bill’s fate
- Unfair decision driven by convenience
- Stakeholders
- Bill Brundle
- Harriet Corrigan
- Betty Hastings
- Department co-workers
- Upper management
- LowRider Corporation
- Military customers

## Slide 17

- Step 2: The Major Ethical Dilemma
- WORKED EXAMPLE
- Should Harriet fire Bill?
- Other dilemmas exist — e.g. should Betty have told Harriet? — but Harriet’s decision is the one that must be made now.

## Slide 18

- Step 3: Consequentialism (Utilitarianism)
- WORKED EXAMPLE
- Fire Bill
- Do not fire Bill
- Harmed
- A. Bill (loses job); department (loses a crucial worker, more disruption); co-workers (lower morale, fear)
- B. Harriet and management (authority questioned); company (security bypass seems tolerated); Betty (loses her job)
- Benefit
- D. Betty (keeps a job); management (strong deterrent against misuse of e-mail and security)
- E. Bill (keeps job); department (keeps expertise, less disruption); Harriet (avoids replacing a key worker)
- C. Least harm: B — not firing (harm can be reduced by discipline)
- F. Most benefit: E — not firing benefits more people

## Slide 19

- Step 3: Rights and Duties (G)
- WORKED EXAMPLE
- Stakeholder
- Right
- Corresponding duty
- Bill
- Fair treatment and due process before dismissal
- Harriet’s duty to investigate fairly and apply the same rules as for others
- Management
- To be treated with respect and not impersonated
- Bill’s duty not to misrepresent or mock them
- LowRider
- Secure, properly used computer systems
- Bill’s duty not to bypass security; staff duty to follow e-mail policy
- Co-workers
- A workplace free of offensive material
- Bill’s duty to communicate respectfully
- Harriet
- Honest answers from staff
- Bill’s and Betty’s duty of honesty
- Betty
- Fair consideration for available jobs
- Harriet’s duty to judge Bill on his conduct, not on Betty’s convenience

## Slide 20

- Step 3: Kant’s Categorical Imperative
- WORKED EXAMPLE
- Question
- Fire Bill
- Do not fire Bill
- Preferable
- Who is disrespected?
- H. Bill, if used as a mere means to save Betty’s job or make an example
- I. Management and offended staff, if the offence is ignored
- J. Neither is ideal; I can be fixed with discipline and an apology
- Who is treated unlike others?
- K. Bill, if others who broke rules received lesser penalties
- L. None, if Bill gets the penalty any employee would
- M. L
- Universal rule?
- N. “Fire anyone who errs once” — harsh, deters honesty
- O. “Never punish misconduct” — rules lose meaning
- P. Neither extreme: a proportionate penalty can be universal

## Slide 21

- Step 4: A Defensible Decision
- WORKED EXAMPLE
- Harriet should NOT fire Bill — but she should take serious, proportionate disciplinary action.
- Supporting letters: C, F, J, M, P
- Not firing causes the least harm and the most benefit (C, F)
- Firing to free a job for Betty would use Bill as a means to an end (H) — Betty’s situation must not influence the decision
- A proportionate penalty treats Bill as any employee would be treated (M, P)
- Management’s right to respect and the company’s right to security are restored through discipline, apology and security fixes — not only through dismissal

## Slide 22

- Step 4: Implementation and Impact
- WORKED EXAMPLE
- Steps to implement
- 1
- Hold a formal inquiry and hear Bill’s side
- 2
- Issue a final written warning (and suspension if policy allows)
- 3
- Bill apologises to management and staff
- 4
- Remove Bill’s elevated system privileges and patch the flaw
- 5
- Speak to Betty about her silence
- Effect on stakeholders
- Bill: keeps his job but faces real consequences
- Management: authority and respect restored
- Co-workers: see fair, consistent treatment
- Betty: must be considered for other roles on merit
- Company: keeps expertise; systems more secure

## Slide 23

- Step 4: Prevention and the Pivot Point
- WORKED EXAMPLE
- Longer-term changes
- Technical: stronger e-mail authentication; block anonymous sending; audit logs
- Organisational: clear acceptable use policy and disciplinary code
- Social: honest, regular communication and support during layoffs
- Legal: train staff on the Computer Crimes Act and defamation
- The pivot point
- The moment Bill chose to bypass security and sign the memo “Management”. He could have raised morale openly — e.g. a signed, respectful message or a proposal to HR. Management could also have planned the layoffs more fairly and communicated better.

## Slide 25

- Project Instructions
- Choose a case study and dilemma. Solve it using the Four-Step Ethical Decision-Making Process. Every decision must follow the four steps.
- 1
- Understand
- Numbered facts, ethical issues, harms, stakeholders
- 2
- Isolate
- One dilemma: “Should X do Y?” — no reasoning
- 3
- Analyse
- Answer every lettered question A–P
- 4
- Decide
- Decision with letters; steps; impact; prevention; pivot point

## Slide 26

- Questions to Ask When Facing an Ethical Issue
- McFarland (2012)
- 1
- What are the facts?
- 2
- Who are the stakeholders?
- 3
- What alternative responses exist?
- 4
- What are the costs and benefits of each?
- 5
- What rights are at stake, and which would each alternative violate?
- 6
- How are benefits and burdens distributed?
- 7
- Are there useful analogies, and do they really apply?
- 8
- Which laws apply, and are they justifiable?
- 9
- Which professional standards or norms apply?
- 10
- What institutional and social context limits the options?
- 11
- What are the agents’ responsibilities, individually and collectively?

## Slide 27

- Qualities of a Good Ethical Judgement
- Consistent with the facts
- Reasonable, coherent justification
- Based on sound ethical principles
- Benefits outweigh the costs
- Respects autonomy and human rights
- Fair to all; no further harm to the disadvantaged
- Universalisable
- Consistent with laws and norms
- No better alternative exists
- You may not satisfy all of these at once — prioritise the most important and meet as many as possible.

## Slide 28

- Submission Checklist
- Facts are listed and numbered
- Every ethical issue explains why and the harm caused
- All stakeholders are listed
- The dilemma is one question: “Should X do Y?”
- Every lettered question A–P is answered
- Each right is paired with its duty
- Your decision cites the supporting letters
- Implementation steps and stakeholder impact are clear
- Long-term changes and the pivot point are identified
- Laws, codes (ACM, MBOT) and references are cited
