## Slide 4

- The Computer: Two Sides of the Same Tool
- It provides facilities
- Online banking, e-wallets and shopping
- Communication, learning and work
- Government and business services
- It enables crime
- No need to enter a bank to steal money: phishing, cloned cards and hacked accounts
- Crimes can cross borders in seconds
- AI now helps criminals create convincing fakes
- At the same time, the computer serves society and serves criminals.

## Slide 5

- New Crimes, New Challenges
- Tracing
- Criminals hide behind VPNs, fake accounts and cryptocurrency, often in other countries
- Prosecuting
- Evidence is digital, and laws differ from country to country
- Preventing
- Attackers move faster than defenders — some can spread through a network in minutes
- Example: many Malaysian scam syndicates operate from call centres abroad, and more than 800 Malaysians were detained in scam compounds overseas in 2025.

## Slide 6

- Why Does Intrusion Occur?
- Because information security is weak — and the more we connect, the bigger the target.
- Human error
- Staff tricked by phishing or phone calls — e.g. attackers phoned an employee to get login details in the Match Group breach (2026)
- Weak or stolen passwords
- Reused passwords and no multi-factor authentication
- Unpatched systems
- Known software flaws left unfixed
- Misconfigured cloud
- Databases left open online — a 149-million-record database was found exposed in January 2026

## Slide 7

- Does Cybercrime Exist? Defining It
- Cybercrime covers any criminal act involving computers and networks (hacking), plus traditional crimes carried out over the Internet — such as fraud, identity theft, credit-card theft and hate crimes.
- — adapted from Donn B. Parker
- Computer crime
- Uses a computer as a tool, often with direct access — e.g. copying data from a company PC over the office network.
- Cybercrime
- Cannot happen without the Internet — e.g. online scams, phishing websites, illegal content by e-mail or social media.

## Slide 11

- Three Categories of Cybercrime
- 1. Against property
- Theft, fraud, forgery, piracy and malware
- 2. Against people
- Harassment, cyberbullying, illegal content, invasion of privacy
- 3. Cyber terrorism
- Politically motivated attacks on systems and infrastructure

## Slide 12

- 1. Cybercrime Against Property
- Theft
- Stealing information, money or services — e.g. medical records, customer lists, PINs, e-wallet balances
- Fraud
- Lies to make victims hand over money — fake investments, loans, online shops, love scams
- Forgery
- Altering documents or creating fakes — including AI deepfake videos and cloned voices
- Malicious software
- Viruses, ransomware and malicious APK files that take over phones

## Slide 14

- 2. Cybercrime Against People
- Illegal content
- Spreading pornography — especially child sexual abuse material — and non-consensual intimate images, including AI deepfakes
- Cyber harassment & bullying
- Stalking, threats and abuse by e-mail, chat or social media; now a crime under Penal Code s. 507B–507G (2025)
- Doxing
- Publishing someone’s personal information to harm or frighten them
- Invasion of privacy
- Spam, phishing and spyware that compromise and steal information

## Slide 15

- 3. Cyber Terrorism
- Politically motivated attacks against information, computer systems, programs and data — aimed at causing fear, harm or disruption.
- Possible scenarios
- Remotely tampering with food or medicine production to cause illness
- Breaking into air traffic control to change flight routes
- Real attacks on infrastructure
- Stuxnet (discovered 2010): malware that physically damaged Iran’s nuclear centrifuges
- Colonial Pipeline (2021): ransomware shut the largest US fuel pipeline for days
- MAHB (March 2025): Malaysia Airports hit by a cyberattack with a US$10 million ransom demand

## Slide 16

- Computer Crime and Security
- What is security?
- The ability of a system to protect information and resources (CPUs, disks, programs) with respect to confidentiality, integrity and availability — the “CIA triad”
- In general: freedom from risk or danger
- In computer science
- Preventing access to information by unauthorised people
- Preventing deliberate, unauthorised destruction or change of information
- Keeping systems available to legitimate users
- Attackers and intruders turn information into a battlefield — “information warfare”.

## Slide 17

- How and When Was Computer Crime Detected?
- 1950s
- Computers store confidential data; threats: spying and privacy invasion
- Late 1960s
- Remote sharing of systems; ARPANET (1969) creates new security problems
- 1988
- Morris Worm: first major Internet worm, infecting ~10% of connected computers
- 2000s
- Viruses, phishing and online fraud spread with broadband
- 2010s–now
- Ransomware, state hacking, crypto theft and AI-powered scams
- Attack patterns: breaking into systems, erasing audit trails, vandalism, hate speech, disruption, data theft, revenge and sabotage by competitors.

## Slide 19

- Computer Crimes and Malaysian Law
- Computer crime
- Law
- Unauthorised access (hacking)
- Computer Crimes Act 1997, s. 3 (and s. 4 if done to commit another offence)
- Unauthorised modification (e.g. malware, deleting data)
- Computer Crimes Act 1997, s. 5
- Harassment, threats, grossly offensive or fraudulent messages
- Communications and Multimedia Act 1998, s. 233 (amended 2025: up to RM500,000 and/or 2 years)
- Bullying, cyberbullying, doxing
- Penal Code s. 507B–507G (in force 11 July 2025)
- Defamation by e-mail or social media
- Penal Code s. 500; civil defamation claims
- Online fraud and cheating
- Penal Code s. 420
- Attacks on critical infrastructure
- Cyber Security Act 2024
- Harmful content on large platforms
- Online Safety Act 2025 (in force 1 January 2026)

- 2025–2026

## Slide 22

- Recent Cases: Around the World
- 2024–2026
- Change Healthcare (US)
- February 2024
- Ransomware on a health-payments firm disrupted US pharmacies and exposed data on about 190 million people.
- Arup deepfake fraud
- 2024
- A Hong Kong employee joined a video call with deepfaked executives and transferred about US$25 million.
- Bybit crypto theft
- February 2025
- About US$1.5 billion in cryptocurrency stolen — the largest crypto theft ever, attributed by the FBI to North Korea.
- Marks & Spencer (UK)
- April 2025

## Slide 23

- Lessons from the Latest Cases
- People are the weakest link
- Phishing, fake calls and deepfakes trick staff — train everyone
- Protect logins
- Use multi-factor authentication and strong, unique passwords
- Back up and plan
- Offline backups and an incident plan limit ransomware damage
- Verify, then act
- Confirm money requests through a second channel — even from the “boss”

## Slide 24

- Case Study: WikiLeaks
- What is it?
- Launched in 2006 (Sunshine Press), a non-profit organisation founded by Julian Assange
- Aim: publish important news and original source documents on public issues
- Method: a secure, anonymous online drop box for leaked documents
- The 2010 controversy
- Released hundreds of thousands of secret US documents with five newspapers: The Guardian, Le Monde, Der Spiegel, El País and The New York Times
- Included a video of a US helicopter attack that killed civilians, and files on the Iraq and Afghanistan wars

## Slide 25

- WikiLeaks: Ethical or Not?
- UPDATED
- Update: After years in the Ecuadorian embassy and a UK prison, Julian Assange pleaded guilty in June 2024 to one US Espionage Act charge in a plea deal, and was freed to return to Australia.
- WikiLeaks’ claim
- Combines strong security technology with journalism and ethical principles
- Exposes government wrongdoing in the public interest
- Critics’ view
- Naïve, reckless and dangerous (Weineke, 2010, as cited in the original slides)
- Disclosures were huge and indiscriminate, may have endangered people, and did not solve the problems exposed

## Slide 26

- Conclusion
- Computers bring great benefits — and new opportunities for crime
- Cybercrime targets property, people and critical infrastructure
- Malaysia has strengthened its laws: CMA amendments, anti-bullying provisions, the Cyber Security Act and the Online Safety Act
- Scams are now the biggest threat to ordinary Malaysians — RM2.9 billion lost in 2025
- Everyone has a role: think before you click, verify before you pay, report to 997
