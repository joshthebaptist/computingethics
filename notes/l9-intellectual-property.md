## Slide 4

- PART 1
- Copyright
- What it protects, who owns it, and how much you may copy

## Slide 5

- What Is Copyright?
- The exclusive right of creators to control the copying and use of original works produced through intellectual effort.
- Protected: the expression
- The way an idea is written, drawn, recorded or coded
- e.g. the actual text of a book, the source code of an app
- Not protected: ideas and facts
- Ideas, facts, methods and raw information
- e.g. the idea of a ride-hailing app — only a specific app’s code and design

## Slide 6

- What Works Are Protected?
- Copyright Act 1987 (Malaysia), section 7
- Literary works
- Books, articles, and computer programs
- Musical works
- Songs and compositions
- Artistic works
- Paintings, drawings, photographs, typography, architecture
- Films
- Movies, videos and broadcasts
- Sound recordings
- Voice and music recordings
- Compilations
- Databases and collections, as derivative works

## Slide 7

- The Dilemma of Copyright Protection
- Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), Article 27
- Article 27(1): Access
- Everyone has the right to freely take part in the cultural life of the community, to enjoy the arts, and to share in scientific advancement and its benefits.
- Article 27(2): Protection
- Everyone has the right to protection of the moral and material interests resulting from any scientific, literary or artistic work they create.
- The conflict: giving the public access to improve wellbeing, education, science and knowledge — while still rewarding creators.

## Slide 8

- Exceptions: Fair Dealing
- The owner’s monopoly does not apply when a work is used fairly for:
- Research and private study
- Non-commercial
- Criticism and review
- With acknowledgement
- Reporting current events
- With acknowledgement
- Teaching and education
- Within limits
- Is it fair? Courts weigh four factors: the purpose of the use (commercial or not), the nature of the work, how much is taken, and the effect on the work’s market value.

## Slide 9

- Who Owns Copyright?
- Most works
- The author (creator) — e.g. the writer of a book
- Made by an employee
- The employer, when the work is made as part of the employee’s job
- Commissioned work
- Usually the person who commissioned and paid for it — unless the contract says otherwise
- Freelancers & contractors
- Decided by the contract — always agree ownership in writing

## Slide 10

- Exclusive Rights of the Owner: Economic Rights
- Reproduce
- Make copies of the work
- Communicate to the public
- Broadcast, stream or put online
- Perform, show or exhibit
- Display the work in public
- Distribute
- Sell or give copies to the public
- Rent or lend
- Commercial rental of copies
- Adapt or translate
- Make modified or translated versions

## Slide 11

- Exclusive Rights of the Owner: Moral Rights
- Copyright Act 1987, section 25 — these stay with the author even after sale
- Right of attribution (paternity)
- To be named as the author of the work
- Right of integrity
- To object to changes that distort or damage the work or the author’s reputation
- Right against false attribution
- Not to be named as the author of something they did not create

## Slide 12

- How Much Can Be Copied?
- Practical guidelines (Pedley) — not fixed legal limits
- Type of work
- Guideline
- Book
- Up to 5% of the work, or one chapter
- Short work of art / anthology
- Up to 10%, as long as the extract is not more than 20 pages
- Journal
- One article from one issue
- Criticism — one extract
- Up to about 400 words
- Criticism — several extracts
- Up to 300 words each, and not more than 800 words in total
- These are cautious rules of thumb from UK library practice. The law itself asks whether the use is fair (see fair-dealing factors).

## Slide 13

- Legal Protection for Computer Programs
- Computer programs are protected as literary works — in source code and object code.
- Berne Convention (1886)
- Basic international copyright treaty; about 180 member countries including Malaysia
- TRIPS Agreement (1994), Art. 10
- WTO members must protect computer programs as literary works
- WIPO Copyright Treaty (1996)
- Confirms protection for software and online distribution
- Copyright Act 1987 (Malaysia)
- Computer programs listed as literary works; 2022 amendment targets illegal streaming devices

## Slide 14

- Data Protection: Handling Personal Data
- Malaysia’s Personal Data Protection Act 2010 — seven principles
- 1
- General
- Process data only with consent, for a lawful purpose
- 2
- Notice & choice
- Tell people why data is collected and let them choose
- 3
- Disclosure
- Don’t disclose data for other purposes without consent
- 4
- Security
- Protect data from loss, misuse and unauthorised access
- 5
- Retention
- Don’t keep data longer than needed
- 6
- Data integrity
- Keep data accurate, complete and up to date
- 7
- Access
- People can see and correct their data

## Slide 15

- A Guide to Using Cyberspace
- Watch out
- Cyberspace raises many issues and can easily trap careless users
- Copying software is a crime
- Copying, sharing or distributing commercial software without a licence breaks the law
- Read the licence
- Before downloading freeware or shareware, check its terms
- Online ≠ free
- Almost everything online is protected by copyright unless it says otherwise

## Slide 16

- When Can You Copy, Post or Use Online Text?
- E-mails and messages
- The writer owns the copyright in their e-mail or message
- Don’t forward, repost or edit it as if it were yours
- You may quote or summarise part of it to comment or criticise
- Public domain & open licences
- Public domain works (copyright expired or waived) can be used freely
- Creative Commons works can be used under the licence terms — usually with credit
- Never use or scan other people’s photos without permission

- Patent

## Slide 18

- What Is a Patent?
- Origin of the word
- From the Latin patere, “to lay open” for public inspection. Letters patent were open royal orders granting someone an exclusive right.
- First patent law
- The Venetian Patent Statute of 1474 protected new devices from being copied — the first systematic patent law.
- Definition: a set of exclusive rights granted by a country for a limited time, in exchange for publicly disclosing the details of a new device, method, process or composition. Others may not make, use, sell or import the invention without permission.

## Slide 19

- The Three Criteria for a Patent
- Patents Act 1983 (Malaysia) and most countries worldwide
- 1. New (novel)
- Not revealed or made public anywhere before the filing date
- 2. Inventive step
- Not obvious to an expert skilled in that field
- 3. Industrially applicable
- Can be made or used in some kind of industry
- In return, the inventor must fully disclose how the invention works, so experts can reproduce it.

## Slide 20

- Why Do We Need Patents?
- To share knowledge
- Without patents, inventors keep inventions secret. Patents make the details public, and anyone can use them once protection expires.
- To reward R&D
- Patents motivate efficient research and development. Without them, companies may avoid investing for fear of being copied.

## Slide 21

- The Dilemma of Software Patents
- For
- Protects creators and innovators
- Stops ideas from being stolen
- Gives creators a financial reward
- Attracts investment in software R&D
- Against
- May slow down ICT development
- Big companies can use patents against small ones, killing innovation — e.g. “patent trolls”
- Can threaten open-source projects
- Software is often seen as maths or logic, which cannot be patented

## Slide 22

- Patent Issues and Myths
- Is software patentable?
- Software “as such” is excluded in many places (e.g. Europe), but computer-implemented inventions with a technical effect can be patented; US courts narrowed software patents in Alice (2014)
- Costly and complex
- Filing, search and examination fees, annual renewal fees and patent-agent costs
- Territorial
- A patent only protects you in the country where it is granted — but the PCT system lets you file one international application covering about 158 countries
- First to file
- Each country grants the patent to the first applicant; the Paris Convention gives 12 months’ priority to file abroad

## Slide 23

- Before Applying for a Patent
- Search existing patents first
- Use free databases: WIPO PATENTSCOPE, Google Patents, MyIPO’s online search
- Avoid wasting money, time and effort on something that is not patentable
- Avoid accidentally copying an existing invention
- If it is already patented, ask…
- What technology does it use — do you need the same?
- What is its design — can you design around it?
- What materials — can you use different ones?
- What production method — can you use another?
- Or: seek a licence from the owner

## Slide 24

- How Long Does a Patent Last?
- 20
- years from the filing date, if annual fees are paid
- Patents Act 1983, s. 35 · TRIPS Art. 33
- What happens when it expires?
- The invention enters the public domain — anyone can make and use it without paying the owner.
- Example: when drug patents expire, cheaper generic medicines can be made — lowering costs for patients and hospitals.

## Slide 26

- What Is a Trademark?
- Any sign that lets consumers tell one company’s goods or services from another’s
- Logos
- e.g. the Apple logo, the Petronas logo
- Words and names
- e.g. Acer, Dell, Grab
- Slogans
- e.g. KFC’s “Finger lickin’ good”
- Shapes and packaging
- e.g. the Coca-Cola bottle
- Sounds
- e.g. the Windows start-up sound, the Intel chime
- Colours, scents, holograms, motion
- Allowed in Malaysia since the Trademarks Act 2019

## Slide 27

- What Makes a Good Trademark?
- Distinctive
- It must stand out and not simply describe the product (e.g. “Sweet” for sugar cannot be registered)
- Not confusing
- It must not be similar to an existing mark, which could confuse buyers — e.g. counterfeit “Abibas” shoes imitating Adidas
- Not deceptive
- It must not trick users about the product’s origin or quality
- Protected for 10 years
- Renewable every 10 years, for as long as it is used

- PART 4
- IP in the Age of AI
- New questions for copyright and patents

## Slide 29

- Can AI Be an Author or an Inventor?
- NEW
- AI as inventor (DABUS)
- Stephen Thaler tried to name his AI system DABUS as an inventor. Courts in the UK (Supreme Court, 2023) and the US refused: an inventor must be a human
- AI as author
- In Thaler v. Perlmutter (2025), a US appeals court upheld that a work created entirely by AI cannot be copyrighted — human authorship is required
- Training on copyrighted works
- Authors and artists are suing AI companies. In Bartz v. Anthropic, a US court found training on books was fair use but downloading pirated copies was not; a US$1.5 billion settlement was approved in 2026
- For you as a student
- If you use AI, you may not own what it produces — and you must acknowledge it

## Slide 30

- Summary
- Copyright, patents and trademarks are all ways of protecting intellectual property — each balancing rewards for creators with benefits for society.
- Copyright
- Protects expression · automatic · life + 50 years
- Patent
- Protects inventions · registered · 20 years
- Trademark
- Protects brands · registered · 10 years, renewable
