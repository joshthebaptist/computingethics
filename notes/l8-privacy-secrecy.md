## Slide 4

- Information Security, Secrecy and Privacy
- Information Security
- is closely related to
- Secrecy
- Keeping sensitive information of governments, companies and businesses confidential
- Privacy
- An individual’s control over information about themselves

## Slide 5

- When Secrecy Is Lost, Privacy Is Lost
- An intruder breaks into a system
- Information loses its secrecy
- Loss of secrecy leads to loss of privacy
- People’s lives are disrupted
- Example: In 2017, personal data of about 46 million Malaysian mobile subscribers was leaked online — putting millions at risk of scams and identity theft.

## Slide 6

- What Is Needed to Prevent Privacy Breaches?
- Law
- — supported by technology and good practice
- Why law?
- To balance what individuals can expect about how their information is kept safe with the information they give in exchange for services.
- You share data to get a service (a bank account, an app, a job)
- In return, the organisation must protect it and use it fairly
- The law sets and enforces this bargain

## Slide 7

- What Are Privacy and Secrecy?
- Privacy
- Concerns information about an individual — who they are, what they do, and what they want to keep to themselves.
- Secrecy
- Concerns sensitive information about governments, corporations and businesses — equity, products, services and plans.
- Privacy protects people. Secrecy protects organisations. Both depend on information security.

## Slide 8

- What Is Private Information?
- Personal identity
- Full name, IC number, address, phone, marital status, income
- Private communication
- Calls, chats and messages meant to stay private
- Health & medical
- Medical history and current conditions
- Private activities
- Diet, social life, alcohol use, hobbies, clubs, firearm ownership
- Biometric data
- Fingerprints, face scans, voice — now “sensitive data” under Malaysia’s PDPA
- Digital traces
- Location history, browsing, cookies and app usage

## Slide 9

- The Privacy Dilemma
- Private information must be protected
- People have a right to control their own data
- Misuse causes harm: scams, discrimination, embarrassment
- But data businesses are growing
- Credit bureaus (e.g. CTOS), data brokers and ad networks trade in personal information
- Apps and platforms earn money from targeted ads
- The challenge: protect one person’s privacy without unfairly blocking the legitimate interests of others.

## Slide 10

- Privacy Can Be Lost Without Us Knowing
- Low awareness
- New Internet users often don’t understand privacy risks
- Few read privacy policies or check app permissions
- Silent violations
- Information you give for one purpose may be shared and kept for other uses
- e.g. a “free” quiz app that collects your contacts, or a lucky-draw form sold to marketers

## Slide 11

- The Moral Right to Privacy
- We have the right to control our confidential and private information — BUT technology threatens this right.
- Watching
- Computers can monitor a person’s life, habits, location and associations — e.g. CCTV with facial recognition
- Collecting
- Information is easy to collect, store and combine
- Spreading
- It is easy to copy and distribute, and hard to delete

## Slide 12

- Defining Privacy
- General
- The right to be left alone, to be in a state of solitude, and not to be disturbed (Warren & Brandeis, 1890)
- Information technology
- The right of individuals to decide when, how and to what extent information about them is shared (Westin, 1967)
- Purpose limitation
- Information collected for one purpose should not be used for another without consent
- Law: intrusion
- Protection from unwanted intrusion, e.g. tracking of web-browsing activity
- Law: identity misuse
- Protection from unauthorised use of your name or identity, e.g. credit-card fraud
- Law: publicity
- Protection from unwanted publicity, e.g. revealing someone’s illness
- Law: false light
- Protection from publication of false information about a person

## Slide 13

- PART 2
- Threats to Privacy
- Who keeps our data, and when it is at risk

## Slide 14

- How Computers Expose Private Data
- Online payments
- Card and e-wallet transactions leave a digital trail in databases that other parties may access or misuse
- Weak points in transactions
- Poorly secured systems leave “back doors” for attackers
- Government records
- Tax, voter lists, medical, zakat, firearm licences and MyKad data are held by government agencies
- Company records
- Banks, lenders, hospitals, telcos, e-commerce and delivery apps hold large amounts of personal data

## Slide 15

- Why Do Organisations Keep Personal Information?
- Government agencies
- To deliver services efficiently
- To check eligibility, e.g. for jobs or aid
- To track debtors
- To detect fraud
- Businesses
- Banks: transactions and balances
- Telcos: call records and location data
- Insurers: claim records
- Platforms: behaviour data for targeted ads
- Some share or sell data for marketing — often without clear consent

## Slide 16

- Personal Information Is at Risk When…
- Used without consent
- Sold or used for commercial purposes without permission
- Misused by insiders
- System administrators access or use it illegally
- Wrong information spreads
- False data about a person is distributed
- Leaked by negligence
- Poor security lets unauthorised people retrieve it
- Today, misconfigured cloud storage and phishing are among the most common causes of data breaches.

## Slide 17

- Personal Data: From Secret to Commodity
- Personal information is no longer a secret — it has become something to buy and sell.
- Why?
- Historically, too few rules and weak laws — especially for medical records
- Business models built on free services paid for by data and advertising
- Laws are catching up
- EU GDPR (2018): fines up to €20 million or 4% of global turnover — Meta was fined €1.2 billion in 2023
- Malaysia’s PDPA was strengthened in 2024

## Slide 19

- Activity: Profiling, Rights and Values
- What is profiling?
- Automatically processing personal and non-personal data
- To build predictive knowledge for decision-making
- Refers to potential (groups of) individuals, e.g. “likely to default on a loan”
- What is a digital persona?
- A digital representation of a real individual (Clarke, 1994)
- Contains enough data to stand in for that person
- Refers to a specific individual
- Discuss: 1. How does profiling affect a person’s fundamental rights and society’s values? 2. Does the online context weaken those rights?

## Slide 20

- When Profiles Become Personal
- When a profile is first created, it does not identify a specific person
- Once a profile is matched to someone — becoming a digital persona — it affects their privacy and data rights directly
- AI now makes profiling faster and more powerful: credit scoring, job screening, targeted political ads
- Case: Cambridge Analytica
- In 2018 it was revealed that data from up to 87 million Facebook users had been harvested through a quiz app and used to build political profiles. Facebook was later fined US$5 billion by the US FTC (2019).

## Slide 21

- Controlling Personal Privacy
- To preserve privacy and freedom, privacy-rights groups say these must come first:
- Keep only relevant information
- Organisations should collect and keep only the data they need (data minimisation).
- Watch the watchers
- Monitor how governments and companies use citizens’ data, to keep them accountable.
- Examples of such groups: EFF, Privacy International, and Malaysia’s Sinar Project.

## Slide 22

- The OECD Privacy Principles
- Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development — 1980, updated 2013
- 1
- Collection limitation
- Collect lawfully, fairly and with consent
- 2
- Data quality
- Keep data accurate, complete and relevant
- 3
- Purpose specification
- State the purpose when collecting
- 4
- Use limitation
- Don’t use data for other purposes without consent
- 5
- Security safeguards
- Protect data from loss and misuse
- 6
- Openness
- Be open about data practices
- 7
- Individual participation
- People can see and correct their data
- 8
- Accountability
- The data controller is responsible

## Slide 23

- The Challenge of Protecting Privacy
- There is no absolute privacy. Humans are social and depend on society, so some personal information must be shared with others.
- Why? It is the price of belonging to society: society expects something in return for the benefits it gives.
- Information must flow
- Sharing information is needed to get tasks done — it cannot be avoided.
- People need private space
- Individuals need room to grow privately, so some information must never be made public.

## Slide 24

- Code of Fair Information Practices
- US Department of Health, Education and Welfare (1973)
- 1
- People can find out what data is held about them and how it is used
- 2
- No personal data record-keeping system may be kept secret
- 3
- People can correct or amend records about them
- 4
- Organisations that create, maintain, use or share data are responsible for its security and reliability
- 5
- Data collected for one purpose cannot be used for another without consent
- Principles 2 and 4 are controversial: few people realise how little in a system is truly secret. Notifying everyone raises costs.

- Law & Surveillance
- Privacy laws today, and the debate over government spying

## Slide 26

- Malaysia: Personal Data Protection Act 2010
- Amended in 2024 — the biggest reform since it came into force in 2013
- Higher penalties
- Up to RM1 million fine and/or 3 years’ jail for breaching data protection principles (from 1 April 2025)
- Biometric data
- Fingerprints and facial data are now “sensitive personal data”
- Breach notification
- Data controllers must report breaches to the Commissioner, and tell affected people if harm is likely (from 1 June 2025)
- Data protection officers
- Controllers and processors must appoint a DPO (from 1 June 2025)
- Data portability
- People can ask for their data to be moved to another provider

## Slide 27

- Spying and Surveillance
- Spying is one way privacy is invaded — used by governments, law enforcement, businesses, spies and politicians.
- Wiretapping in the US
- Katz v. United States (1967): phone calls are protected by the Fourth Amendment; since 1968, police need a court warrant to tap calls
- Mass surveillance
- Edward Snowden’s 2013 leaks revealed US NSA programmes collecting phone records and Internet data on a huge scale
- Encryption fights
- Apple vs FBI (2016): should companies unlock encrypted phones for investigators?

## Slide 28

- The Debate: Privacy vs Security
- Support government access
- Unlimited access to information protects the public from drugs, crime and terrorism
- Fear of abuse
- Governments may abuse special powers; intercepting private communication threatens freedom
- A useful tool
- Wiretapping detects dangerous plots early and helps prove illegal financial transactions
- Where should the line be drawn — and who should decide?

## Slide 29

- How Do We View Computer Crime?
- Our view of computer crime has not yet merged with society’s view of other property crimes: we have laws against breaking and entering, but they are not widely applied to computer crimes.
- Is this still true today?
- Malaysia’s Computer Crimes Act 1997 treats unauthorised access like “digital trespass”
- The Cyber Security Act 2024 and Online Safety Act 2025 add new duties
- Yet many people still see hacking into an account as less serious than breaking into a house

## Slide 30

- Protecting Your Own Privacy
- NEW
- Lock your accounts
- Strong, unique passwords and multi-factor authentication
- Check app permissions
- Turn off location, contacts and microphone access you don’t need
- Manage cookies
- Reject non-essential cookies; review privacy settings
- Guard your IC number
- Don’t share it in lucky draws or unknown forms
- Use your PDPA rights
- Ask organisations what data they hold, and correct it
- Watch for phishing
- Breached data is used to make scams look real
