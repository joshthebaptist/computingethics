## Slide 4

- What Is Epistemology?
- From the Greek epistēmē (knowledge) + logos (study)
- Epistemology is the branch of philosophy that studies the nature, sources, scope and limits of knowledge — how it relates to truth, belief and justification, and how we respond to scepticism about knowledge claims.
- Traditional view
- Investigates the methods, validity and scope of knowledge
- Asks which knowledge claims are justified (right) and which are not (wrong)
- Classic definition: knowledge as “justified true belief” (Plato)
- Modern view
- Used in cognitive science: the mind as an information processor
- Shapes psychology and artificial intelligence research
- Underpins how computer programs and AI systems represent “knowledge”

## Slide 5

- What Is Knowledge? Four Classic Views
- Greek philosophers
- Plato, Aristotle
- Knowledge as absolute, universal awareness that is independent of the individual knower
- Pragmatism
- Peirce, James, Dewey
- Knowledge as practical models of the world that help us solve problems; true if it works
- Rationalism
- Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz
- Knowledge as a product of reason and rational reflection, not the senses alone
- Empiricism
- Locke, Berkeley, Hume
- Knowledge as a product of sensory experience, observation and experiment

## Slide 9

- How Did the Computer Start?
- Alan Turing (1912–1954): British mathematician, logician and code-breaker
- 1936
- “On Computable Numbers”
- Introduces the Turing machine to answer Hilbert’s decision problem (Entscheidungsproblem)
- 1939–45
- Bletchley Park
- Helps break the German Enigma cipher during World War II
- 1950
- The Turing Test
- “Computing Machinery and Intelligence” asks: can machines think?
- Today
- Legacy
- Every modern computer is, in principle, a universal Turing machine
- Core idea: information is turned into syntax — basic symbols such as 1 and 0 — which a machine manipulates by fixed rules (algorithms) to produce new information.

## Slide 10

- Who Is the “Turing Man”?
- Term from J. David Bolter, Turing’s Man (1984)
- A human who adopts the characteristics of the computer — and a computer given the characteristics of a human
- Able to work, “think” and monitor: capabilities that seem to require intelligence
- Seen as the ideal person of the digital age
- In January 1983, TIME named the computer its “Machine of the Year” for 1982 instead of a Person of the Year
- Bolter: the Turing Man…
- Thinks in computing logic
- Turing logic
- Speaks in computer language
- Turing language
- Sees reality as information symbols
- Turing world

## Slide 11

- Describing an object with human traits sounded strange or unpleasant.
- Computer age

- Examples then and now
- ELIZA (1966)
- Joseph Weizenbaum’s chatbot imitating a psychotherapist
- WillMaker
- An early expert system that helps people write a legal will
- AI chatbots (2022–)

## Slide 12

- Syntax vs Semantics: A Crucial Difference
- People may forget that computing and human contexts differ
- Computer context
- Information is syntax only — the arrangement of symbols, words and sentences according to rules.
- Human context
- Information is both syntactic and semantic — it carries meaning, intention and value.
- Discussion: What is the long-term effect of giving human characteristics to computers? Does a large language model that writes fluent text actually understand meaning? (See John Searle’s “Chinese Room”, 1980.)

## Slide 13

- Can work without help and even change its own instructions
- Does far more than “crunch numbers” — not bound to one task

## Slide 16

- Turing Technology and the Natural World
- Secular science changed modern people’s perception and world view
- A world full of meaning and inspiring values is reduced by a syntactic approach to quantities and numbers
- The Turing world: an information space represented as numbers or physical quantities (energy, mass)
- Turing language: instructions built from symbols, processes and algorithms, so the world can be transferred to machines
- The risk
- Objects are reduced to their syntactic dimension. Inner meaning is ignored — and this may eventually be treated as the underlying truth.

## Slide 19

- Thinkers on Technology and Social Change
- Thorstein Veblen
- 1857–1929 · US economist & sociologist
- Widely credited with the idea of technological determinism
- Society evolves through inventing and accepting new technologies
- Technology is the material base that sets a society’s status
- Marshall McLuhan
- 1911–1980 · Canadian media theorist
- “The medium is the message” (1964); coined the “global village”
- Tools take on a life of their own; society reacts to them
- Warned the global village would not be peaceful; foresaw digital relationships
- Alvin Toffler
- 1928–2016 · US futurist
- Future Shock (1970): stress from “too much change in too short a time”
- The Third Wave (1980): agricultural → industrial → information society
- Predicted working from home and information overload

## Slide 30

- Where Ethics Began
- Systematic ethics began about 2,400 years ago in ancient Greece
- Socrates (c. 470–399 BC) wrote nothing down; his student Plato recorded his ideas
- In Plato’s Crito, the imprisoned Socrates uses ethical reasoning to explain why he accepts an unjust death sentence rather than escape to his family
- Many ethical theories were developed over the following centuries
- Subjective relativism
- Protagoras, 5th c. BC
- Cultural relativism
- Sumner, Benedict, 20th c.
- Divine command theory
- Ancient; examined in Plato’s Euthyphro
- Ethical egoism
- Hobbes; Ayn Rand
- Kantianism
- Immanuel Kant, 1785
- Act utilitarianism
- Bentham 1789; Mill 1861
- Rule utilitarianism
- 20th-century refinement
- Social contract theory
- Hobbes 1651; Rousseau; Rawls 1971

## Slide 31

- What Makes a Good Ethical Theory?
- A sound theory lets its followers examine moral problems, reach decisions, and defend those decisions to sceptical but open-minded people.
- Scenario: a debate before a neutral audience
- 1
- You say an action is right; I disagree and ask: “Why do you think so?”
- 2
- If you cannot give logical reasons, you will convince no one
- 3
- If you give reasons based on facts and values the audience accepts, you may persuade them
- The public rejects ethical theories that are not based on reasons derived from accepted facts or values.

## Slide 32

- Case: Children and Inappropriate Content
- The concern
- Parents want to protect children from pornographic and violent material. The concern moved from websites, to camera phones and “sexting”, to today’s social media, livestreams and AI-generated deepfakes.
- Web filters
- Software that runs in the background, checks each page the browser tries to load, and blocks pages it judges objectionable.
- Recent laws
- UK Online Safety Act 2023 (age checks from July 2025) · Australia’s social media minimum age of 16 (in force December 2025) · Malaysia’s Online Safety Act 2025 (in force 1 January 2026)

## Slide 33

- Ethical Evaluation of CIPA
- Children’s Internet Protection Act (US, 2000); upheld by the Supreme Court in 2003
- Kantian (deontological)
- Actions are judged by duty and principle, not results
- Goal: protect children — good
- Means: filters that also block legitimate pages
- Authors of blocked content never consented — they are treated merely as a means to an end
- Conclusion: CIPA is hard to justify
- Act utilitarian
- Actions are judged by consequences (consequentialism)
- Benefit: fewer children exposed to pornography
- Harm: library computers less useful for research
- Harm: adults asking to unblock may be stigmatised
- Conclusion: depends on how benefits and harms are weighed

## Slide 34

- Deontology vs Utilitarianism in Health Care
- Deontological
- Utilitarian
- Core idea
- The morality of an action depends on its nature; harm is wrong whatever the outcome
- Outcomes justify the means: the greatest good for the greatest number
- In practice
- The doctor–patient relationship; breaching this duty can amount to medical negligence
- Limited money, time and energy are allocated for the best care for society
- Typical holders
- Doctors and medical staff
- Public health experts, hospital managers, politicians
- Example
- Protecting patient confidentiality, even from insurers
- Oral polio vaccine: rare vaccine-linked paralysis accepted for mass protection
