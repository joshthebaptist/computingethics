## Slide 5

- Principles Often Used as a Guide
- An action tends to be judged ethical when it passes these tests
- Universal benefit
- Would it still be good if everyone in society did the same thing? Does it benefit many?
- Justice
- Does it treat others fairly and give each person what they are due?
- Respect
- Does it respect other people’s feelings, dignity and rights?

## Slide 6

- What Is Ethics?
- Ethics is the philosophical study of morality: a rational examination of people’s moral beliefs and behaviour, concerned with what is good for individuals and for society.
- It helps us…
- Live a good life
- Become a better person
- Do the right thing
- Live well with others

- The questions it covers

- What are my rights and responsibilities?

- How do I decide what is good or bad?
- Where our ideas of ethics come from

- Applied ethics

## Slide 9

- What Is the Use of Ethics?
- To guide behaviour
- Ethical theories are only useful if they affect how people actually behave.
- Reason should lead to action
- Some philosophers argue that if you know something is morally good, it would be irrational not to do it.
- But people are not always rational
- We often follow our “gut instinct” even when our head suggests a different course.
- A toolkit for thinking
- Even so, ethics gives us good tools for thinking clearly about moral issues.

## Slide 10

- Ethics from an Islamic (and Religious) Perspective
- Known as akhlak — habits, customs, dignity and the character of a person’s inner self
- Akhlak is a firmly rooted state of the soul that moves a person to do good and reject evil, so that good actions flow easily without long deliberation.
- Natural (innate)
- Some good qualities come naturally, such as a readiness to forgive.
- Acquired through training
- Stage 1: careful consideration and reflection
- Stage 2: repeated practice until the good deed becomes a habit

## Slide 11

- On What Basis Do We Judge Good or Bad?
- Humans need a principle to justify whether an action is good or bad
- Secular
- Reason and human wellbeing without religious authority
- Religion
- Revealed guidance and scripture
- Law
- Rules enforced by the state
- Dogma

## Slide 12

- Ethics Is Based on Reasons
- Individuals must explain why they hold a view
- This lets us compare different ethical evaluations
- When two people reach different conclusions, we examine the facts and the reasoning to find the stronger rationale
- Example: a public debate
- You say an action is right; I say it is wrong. I ask: “Why do you think it is right?”
- No logical reasons
- You influence no one — no acceptable ethical basis
- Clear reasons
- You may convince the audience; at least your facts and values can be debated — ethical

## Slide 13

- The Moral Realm: Voluntary Choices
- Ethics focuses on voluntary moral choices — not on actions forced on us or outside the moral realm
- Choosing a car colour
- Not a moral issue: no benefit or harm to anyone else.
- Outside the moral realm
- Swerving to avoid a pedestrian
- You lose control and fatally hit another person. Harm is caused, but it was a reflex, not a choice.
- Not an ethical decision
- Driving under the influence
- You chose to drink or take drugs before driving, and that choice harmed others.
- Inside the moral realm

## Slide 15

- Three Sources of Ethics
- 1. Intuition
- Knowing without long study or reasoning
- Some experts treat it as a valid test of right and wrong

- It can vary with time and circumstance
- 2. Custom

- Traditions carry a sense of the sacred (weddings, births, caring for the dead)
- Beliefs and practices are inherited from ancestors
- 3. Theology
- Good and bad are judged by obedience to God’s commands
- Schools of theology exist in Islam, Christianity, Buddhism and Hinduism

## Slide 17

- Ethics versus Morals
- A common working distinction — philosophers often use the two words interchangeably
- Ethics
- Morals
- Definition
- Guiding principles of conduct for an individual or group
- A person’s own principles of right and wrong
- Influenced by
- Profession, field, organisation
- Society, culture and religion
- Link to work
- Often tied to professional roles and codes
- Personal, not tied to a job
- Consistency
- Fairly uniform within a group
- Vary across cultures and religions
- How formed
- Developed deliberately over time
- Often felt intuitively

## Slide 18

- Morals and Ethics in Practice
- Morals: keep promises, be forgiving, don’t cheat, tell the truth, be generous
- Ethics: honesty, loyalty, respect, fairness, integrity; professional codes (doctors, lawyers, engineers, ACM)
- When they conflict
- Lawyer
- Moral: murder is wrong and should be punished.
- Ethics: must defend the client as well as possible, even if believed guilty.
- Doctor
- Moral: may believe a suffering patient has a right to die.
- Ethics: follows professional and legal rules. Euthanasia is illegal in Malaysia, though a few countries (e.g. Netherlands, Canada) allow it.

## Slide 19

- Immoral, Unethical, or Both?
- Immoral
- A politician uses power to free a brother who committed a crime
- It protects a wrongdoer, going against personal standards of right and wrong.
- Unethical
- A manager appoints a brother as supplier without a fair process
- Supplier selection must be transparent and unbiased (conflict of interest).
- Both
- Selling pirated software, or AI-generated fake reviews, to earn more
- It cheats customers and breaks professional codes of conduct.

## Slide 20

- Morals vs Ethics vs Law
- Source: Reynolds, Ethics in Information Technology (6th ed., 2019)
- Morals
- A person’s own beliefs about right and wrong
- Ethics
- Standards or codes of behaviour a group expects of its members
- Law
- A system of rules, enforced by institutions, that says what we can and cannot do
- Legal acts conform to the law. Moral acts conform to what a person believes is right. The two do not always match.

- Descriptive, normative and meta-ethics; deontology vs teleology

## Slide 23

- Four Levels of Ethics Theory
- 1. Descriptive
- Describes the moral systems people actually hold — e.g. how Muslims, Christians, Buddhists and Hindus understand morality. It does not judge.
- 2. Normative
- Judges existing systems and proposes the standard everyone should follow. Practical: it can say an action is right or wrong.
- 3. Meta-ethics
- Studies what cannot be seen directly: the meaning of moral words and the nature of moral facts, drawing on psychology, metaphysics and linguistics.
- 4. Applied
- Applies theory to individual, social and work life — including computer ethics: privacy, AI, cybercrime and IP.

## Slide 26

- Teleology: Judging by Results
- From the Greek telos = end, goal or purpose
- An action is right if it produces good results, and wrong if it causes harm or misery
- Also called consequentialism; utilitarianism is its best-known form
- Looks at past experience to predict the likely results of a present action
- Tech example: some argue electronic workplace monitoring is justified if it prevents fraud, even though it limits privacy and freedom
- Key question
- “What will happen if I do this, and who benefits or is harmed?”

## Slide 27

- Deontology: Judging by Duty
- From the Greek deon = duty + logos = study
- Focuses on the link between duty and the morality of an action
- An action is good because of what it is, not because its results are good
- Some acts are required (or forbidden) regardless of consequences
- Sayings: “Duty for duty’s sake” and “Virtue is its own reward”
- Best-known thinker: Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)
- Key question
- “What is my duty, and would I accept this rule if everyone followed it?”

## Slide 28

- Deontology vs Teleology
- Deontology (duty)
- Teleology (results)
- Also called
- Duty-based ethics
- Results-oriented or consequentialist ethics
- Means and ends
- A good end does not justify wrong means
- The means are judged by the ends they produce
- Guiding rule
- Kant: act only on rules you could will for everyone; treat people as ends, never merely as means
- Greatest happiness principle: most happiness, least pain for the most people
- Teaches
- Be fair; do not use others for selfish ends
- Choose the action with the best overall outcome
- Relies on
- Moral values and duties instilled in each person
- Past experience to predict results

## Slide 29

- Case: Abu Steals from the Rich to Help the Poor
- Teleology (results)
- Weigh all the consequences, not the intention
- Good result: the poor are helped and happier
- Bad results: the rich are harmed; trust and security in society decline
- Verdict depends on the balance — many utilitarians still call it wrong because of long-term harm
- Deontology (duty)
- Stealing takes others’ property without permission
- It breaks a duty regardless of good intentions or results
- Verdict: WRONG
- It is also legally wrong

## Slide 32

- Three Ethical Doctrines
- Nihilism
- Rejects values and knowledge; life has no inherent meaning. Often negative, cynical and pessimistic.
- Hedonism
- Pleasure is the ultimate goal of life and the highest good.
- Existentialism
- Finding oneself and life’s meaning through free will, choice and personal responsibility.

## Slide 33

- 1. Nihilism: Abandoning Values and Knowledge
- From the Latin nihil = nothing (the same root as “annihilate”)
- Labels all values as worthless, so nothing can truly be known or communicated
- Linked to extreme pessimism and radical scepticism, with no loyalties
- The term was popularised by Turgenev’s novel Fathers and Sons (1862); Friedrich Nietzsche warned of its rise
- Choosing “nothingness” has a high price: feeling instead of thinking, and the “will to power” instead of faith, gratitude or obedience to God
- Ethical (moral) nihilism
- Rejects any absolute moral values. Good and evil are vague, and values are simply the result of social and emotional pressure.
- Online example: “nothing matters” trolling culture

## Slide 34

- 2. Hedonism: Pleasure as the Highest Good
- From the Greek hēdonē = pleasure
- Holds that pleasure is the highest moral good and the main goal of life
- Focuses on serving our own needs and seeking enjoyment and happiness as a way of life
- Cyrenaics
- Aristippus, 4th c. BC
- Seek immediate, physical pleasure
- Epicureans
- Epicurus, 341–270 BC
- Seek calm, lasting pleasure; use self-control to avoid pain
- Utilitarian link
- Bentham, 1789
- Pleasure and pain as the measure of right and wrong

## Slide 35

- 3. Existentialism: Existence Before Essence
- A 20th-century philosophy centred on human existence
- Humans exist first; each person then spends a lifetime shaping their own essence or nature
- People discover who they are through choices based on experience, beliefs and outlook
- Personal choices are unique and do not depend on one objective truth
- Each person must choose and take responsibility, without relying on laws, rules or traditions to decide for them
- Key thinkers
- Søren Kierkegaard (forerunner)
- Friedrich Nietzsche (forerunner)
- Jean-Paul Sartre — “existence precedes essence” (1946)
- Simone de Beauvoir
- Albert Camus

## Slide 36

- Existentialism: What It Accepts and Rejects
- Core ideas
- Human free will
- Our nature is chosen through life choices
- We are at our best when struggling for life
- Decisions bring stress and consequences
- Not everything is rational
- Personal responsibility and discipline are crucial
- Society’s rules are arbitrary; worldly desire is futile
- It does not support…
- Wealth, pleasure or honour as the good life
- Social structures controlling the individual
- Accepting things as they are
- Science will make everything better
- People are good but ruined by society
- “I want my way now!” or “It’s not my fault!”

- 2021

- 2024

- Sept 2024
